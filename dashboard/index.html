The Big Picture: What This Project Does

🌌

The Mystery

About 85% of all matter in the universe is dark matter — a mysterious substance that doesn't emit or absorb light. We know it's there because of its gravitational effects, but we can't see it directly. The biggest unsolved question in physics: What IS dark matter?

Different theories predict dark matter behaves in different ways. Some say it's "cold" and clumpy (CDM), others say it's "warm" and smoother (WDM), and others propose exotic particles like ultra-light axions (FDM) or particles that bounce off each other (SIDM). Each theory predicts a different number of small dark matter clumps (called subhalos) orbiting our galaxy.

⭐

Our Cosmic Detectors

When a globular cluster (a ball of thousands of stars) gets ripped apart by our galaxy's gravity, it stretches into a long, thin ribbon of stars called a stellar stream. Think of it like a trail of breadcrumbs stretching across the sky.

These streams are incredibly useful because they act as gravitational antennas. When an invisible dark matter clump flies past a stream, it tugs on the nearby stars with its gravity, creating a visible gap — a region where stars got pulled apart. It's like an invisible bowling ball knocking aside marbles in a line. By counting these gaps and measuring their shapes, we can figure out how many dark matter clumps exist, which tells us what type of dark matter the universe is made of.

🧠

Our Approach

We use artificial intelligence (specifically, a type called a Graph Neural Network) to analyze the positions and motions of every star in a stream. Instead of reducing the data down to simple statistics like previous work did, our AI looks at the full 3D structure — how every star relates to its neighbors, how fast they're moving, and in what direction.

We train this AI on 40,000 simulated streams where we know the answer (because we made them), then apply it to real streams observed by the Gaia space telescope. The AI outputs a probability distribution telling us which dark matter model best explains what we see. This is the first project to combine graph neural networks with simulation-based inference for this problem.

📈

Why It Matters

If we can determine the properties of dark matter, it would be one of the most important discoveries in the history of physics. It would tell us about fundamental particles, the formation of galaxies, and the ultimate fate of the universe.

Our project doesn't just pick one dark matter model — it places model-independent constraints on a single parameter called the half-mode mass (Mhm), which tells us the smallest dark matter clumps that can exist. A low Mhm means lots of small clumps (CDM is right). A high Mhm means small clumps are suppressed (alternative models are right). This makes our results useful regardless of which specific theory turns out to be correct.

Science Background

The Dark Matter Substructure Problem

In plain English: The leading theory (CDM) predicts that our galaxy should be orbited by billions of tiny invisible dark matter clumps. Alternative theories predict far fewer. If we can count these clumps, we can figure out which theory is correct.

Cold Dark Matter (CDM) predicts a rich hierarchy of subhalos orbiting the Milky Way, with the mass function following dN/dM ~ M-1.9 down to Earth-mass scales (Springel et al. 2008). This means far more small subhalos than large ones — for every one subhalo weighing 10 billion solar masses, there should be roughly 80 weighing 1 billion and about 6,000 weighing 100 million.

Alternative models like Warm DM, Fuzzy DM, and Self-Interacting DM suppress this subhalo population below a characteristic half-mode mass Mhm. The half-mode mass is the mass scale where the number of subhalos drops to half of what CDM predicts. Think of it as a "cutoff weight" — below this weight, dark matter clumps can't form.

Stellar streams — tidally disrupted globular clusters or dwarf galaxies stretched into thin arcs — act as gravitational antennas. When a dark matter subhalo flies past a stream, it creates density gaps (regions with fewer stars) and velocity kicks (stars pushed to unusual speeds) in the stream's phase space.

Key insight: More dark matter clumps = more gaps in streams. CDM predicts 5–7 detectable gaps in a stream like GD-1. WDM might predict only 1–2. By counting gaps across multiple streams, we can tell these theories apart.

Why Graph Neural Networks?

Traditional approaches (Bovy+2017, Banik+2021) reduce the 6D phase-space data to 1D power spectra, discarding crucial spatial and kinematic correlations. Imagine taking a detailed photograph and reducing it to just a list of how many pixels are each shade of gray — you lose all the spatial structure.

Our GNN operates directly on the phase-space graph, preserving:

  • Local kinematic coherence — stars near a gap move differently from undisturbed stars
  • Gap morphology — the depth, width, and asymmetry of each gap encodes the mass and speed of the impacting subhalo
  • Velocity dispersion patterns — stars near a recent perturbation show characteristic "heating" signatures
  • Multi-scale clustering — CDM creates many small gaps close together; WDM creates few big ones far apart

GNN Architecture: GINEConv Encoder

What is a Graph Neural Network (GNN)? A regular neural network processes data arranged in a grid (like an image). But our data — thousands of stars scattered in space — doesn't fit a grid. Instead, we connect each star to its 16 nearest neighbors, forming a graph (a network of nodes and connections). The GNN then passes messages along these connections: each star learns about its neighbors, then its neighbors' neighbors, and so on. After 6 rounds of message-passing, every star has information about the entire stream, and we combine all of that into a single 128-number "fingerprint" (embedding) of the whole stream.

Why GINEConv?

GIN (Graph Isomorphism Network) is provably maximally expressive among message-passing GNNs (Xu et al. 2019). The GINE variant incorporates edge features during aggregation, capturing the kinematic delta signals between neighboring stars that encode subhalo perturbations.

Translation: GINEConv is the most powerful type of graph layer available. It's mathematically proven to distinguish between any two different graph structures — meaning it won't accidentally confuse a perturbed stream with an unperturbed one.

Multi-Task Training

The embedding feeds two heads: (1) 3-class classifier (CDM+SIDM / WDM / FDM) and (2) regression (log10Msub, n_impacts). Multi-task training forces the embedding to encode both discrete identity and continuous physical parameters.

Translation: We train the AI to do two jobs at once: classify which type of dark matter it sees, AND estimate specific numbers (how heavy the impacting subhalo was, how many impacts happened). This makes the learned fingerprint much richer than if it only did one task.

vs. Ma+2025 (GCN)

The nearest competitor (Ma+2025) uses vanilla GCN which cannot leverage edge features. Our GINEConv processes the relative proper motion deltas (the core perturbation signal) natively. GINEConv's theoretical advantage is its ability to incorporate edge attributes during message passing, which GCN discards.

Translation: The closest competing research uses a simpler type of graph network that ignores the velocity differences between stars — which is exactly where the perturbation signal lives. Our architecture is designed to use this critical information directly.

Node Feature Importance (by category)

Each star is described by 18 numbers. Kinematics (position and velocity) carry the most physics. Stream identity (which of the 7 streams a star belongs to) uses 7 one-hot dimensions so the model can learn stream-specific properties.

Edge Feature Components

Each connection between two stars carries information about how they differ in position and velocity. The optional orbital features (delta_R, delta_z) add galactic cylindrical coordinates, encoding where in the galaxy's disk each star is.

Integrated Features

The framework integrates seven established machine learning and statistical techniques, adapted for stellar stream dark matter detection. None of these techniques are individually novel — attention mechanisms, MC dropout, hierarchical Bayesian models, and multi-scale graphs are all well-established methods. The contribution is their combination and application to this specific astrophysical domain.

Data Pipeline

How does data flow through the system? The pipeline has five stages, each transforming the data into a more useful form. We start with raw telescope observations and end with probability statements about dark matter. Think of it as: observe stars → simulate what dark matter would do → build a graph of connections → AI processes the graph → statistical inference gives us the answer.

6D phase-space (positions + velocities) for 7 stellar streams

The Gaia space telescope has measured precise positions, distances, and motions for nearly 2 billion stars. We extract the ~5,000 stars in each of our 7 target streams.

⚙

We use the galpy library to simulate realistic Milky Way streams. Each simulation includes a realistic galaxy potential, stream formation, and random dark matter subhalo encounters.

Training Simulation Distribution

We create an equal number of simulations (10,000) for each dark matter model. This ensures the AI doesn't become biased toward any particular model during training. Equal representation means equal opportunity to learn each model's distinctive features.

Data Augmentation Strategy

To make the AI more robust, we randomly modify training streams: shift them along the sky, flip them left-to-right, add realistic noise, and sometimes inject baryonic perturbations (gaps caused by normal matter like molecular clouds) into CDM streams. This teaches the AI to focus on genuine dark matter signals.

Simulation-Based Inference

What is Simulation-Based Inference (SBI)? Traditional statistics require a mathematical formula (called a likelihood) that tells you how probable your observations are given some parameters. But for stellar stream perturbations, this formula is impossibly complex — it involves simulating the gravitational interactions of millions of particles. SBI bypasses this entirely: instead of writing down a formula, we simulate thousands of examples, then train a neural network to learn the relationship between parameters and observations directly from the simulations. It's like learning to recognize faces by looking at thousands of examples, rather than trying to write a mathematical equation for what a face looks like.

SNPE-C (Automatic Posterior Transformation)

We use the Sequential Neural Posterior Estimation algorithm (variant C), which is the state-of-the-art SBI method. It learns the full posterior distribution — not just a single "best guess" but a complete probability map showing how likely each parameter value is.

Pipeline (step by step):

  1. Simulate: Pick random dark matter parameters, run a full stream simulation, create a synthetic stream. This gives us one training example where we know both the input (DM params) and output (stream).
  2. Embed: Run the synthetic stream through our GNN to get a 128-dimensional fingerprint. This is the "summary statistic" that captures everything important.
  3. Learn: A Neural Spline Flow (a type of flexible probability model with 8 transformations and 256-d hidden layers) learns to map from fingerprints to parameter distributions.
  4. Infer: Given a real stream, compute its fingerprint, then ask the learned model: "What dark matter parameters most likely produced this fingerprint?"

Multi-Round Training (5 rounds):

Why multiple rounds? Round 1 explores broadly. Each subsequent round focuses simulations near where the answer is likely to be, making each new simulation much more informative. It's like searching for a lost key: first you check every room (round 1), then focus on the room where you heard a jingle (rounds 2–5).
  • Round 1: 5,000 simulations drawn from a broad prior (uniform exploration)
  • Rounds 2-5: 2,000 simulations each, drawn from the current best estimate of the posterior (targeted exploration)
  • Total: 13,000 targeted simulations per stream (far fewer than the ~100,000+ an amortized approach would need)

Neural Spline Flow Architecture

The Neural Spline Flow converts a simple distribution (like a bell curve) into a complex, multi-peaked distribution through a series of 8 learned transformations. Each transformation uses rational-quadratic splines — flexible curves that can model almost any shape. The result is a probability distribution expressive enough to capture the true uncertainty in dark matter parameters.

Hierarchical Bayesian Multi-Stream Model

Why combine multiple streams? Each stream gives us a noisy measurement of dark matter. By combining all 7 streams, we get a much stronger signal — like asking 7 witnesses to describe a suspect instead of just one. But we can't simply average their answers, because each stream has different "hearing" (sensitivity). A long stream like GD-1 catches more dark matter impacts than a short one like Sylgr. The hierarchical model properly accounts for these differences, giving each stream the right amount of influence.

Model Structure

Mhm ~ Uniform(106, 1010 M☉)

For each stream s:

  ns ~ Poisson(λs(Mhm, Ls, Ts))

In plain English: We assume there's ONE true half-mode mass (Mhm) that applies to all streams equally. For each stream, the expected number of dark matter impacts depends on Mhm and the stream's individual properties (length L, age T). The actual number of impacts follows a Poisson distribution (random count with a known average rate).

The expected rate λ integrates the suppressed subhalo mass function weighted by stream geometric cross-section and age:

λ = C ⋅ (L/10kpc) ⋅ (T/5Gyr) ⋅ ∫ [dN/dlog M] ⋅ T(M, Mhm) dlog M

T(M, Mhm) = [1 + (Mhm/M)2]-1

Breaking it down: The rate formula says: longer streams catch more impacts (proportional to length). Older streams have had more time to accumulate impacts (proportional to age). And the integral counts up all the subhalos that exist, weighted by whether they're suppressed by the half-mode mass or not. The transfer function T smoothly transitions from "fully suppressed" (when Mhm >> M) to "no suppression" (when Mhm << M).

The transfer function follows the WDM suppression form from Schneider et al. (2012). The normalization constant C is calibrated so CDM produces ~5 impacts for a GD-1-like stream (60 deg, 5 Gyr), consistent with Bonaca et al. (2019) and Banik et al. (2021).

We run each through the full pipeline without peeking and check: does the AI's uncertainty range actually contain the true answer? If we claim "90% confidence" and the truth falls in our range 85–95% of the time, the system is well-calibrated and we can trust its results on real streams. This is the gold standard for validating any Bayesian inference system.

Dark Matter Models

The four suspects: Physicists have proposed many theories for what dark matter is. We test the four most popular ones. Each predicts a different number and type of dark matter clumps orbiting our galaxy. Think of CDM as "lots of hard clumps," WDM as "fewer but spread-out clumps," FDM as "quantum fuzzy clumps," and SIDM as "soft clumps that bounce off each other."

CDM

Cold Dark Matter — The Standard Model

Analogy: Imagine dark matter as a bag of marbles. CDM says the marbles come in every size from basketballs down to grains of sand — overwhelmingly many tiny ones.
  • Mass function: dN/dM ~ M-1.9 (many more small clumps than big)
  • Density profile: NFW (Navarro-Frenk-White) — cuspy centers
  • Concentration: Ludlow et al. (2016) relation
  • Subhalo mass range: 105–109 M☉ (100K to 1 billion solar masses)
  • Prediction: 3–7 detectable impacts on GD-1

The reference model. No suppression of subhalo mass function at any scale. If CDM is correct, our streams should show the most gaps.

WDM

Warm Dark Matter — The Hot Candidate

Analogy: Like CDM, but the particles moved too fast in the early universe to clump into small structures. Imagine trying to build a sandcastle on a vibrating table — the big lumps stay, but the small grains won't stick.
  • Mass function: CDM power-law with half-mode filter applied
  • Mhm = 1.7×1010 ⋅ (mWDM/keV)-3.33 M☉
  • Suppression: (1 + (Mhm/M)2.7)-0.99/2.7
  • Particle mass prior: 0.5–10 keV (thermal relic)
  • Reference: Lovell et al. (2014), Schneider et al. (2012)

Free-streaming erases small-scale structure. Lighter WDM particles = more suppression = fewer gaps in streams.

FDM

Fuzzy Dark Matter — The Quantum Wave

Analogy: FDM particles are so incredibly light that they behave like waves, not particles. Imagine dark matter as an ocean — you get big swells (large subhalos) but small ripples wash out (no small subhalos), leaving smooth "soliton" cores.
  • Mass function: CDM power-law with Jeans filter (quantum pressure cutoff)
  • MJeans = 1.5×108 ⋅ (maxion/10-22eV)-1.5 M☉
  • Density: soliton core + NFW envelope (unique to FDM)
  • Soliton radius: rc ~ 1.6/(m22 ⋅ (M/109)1/3) kpc
  • Reference: Hui et al. (2017), Schive et al. (2014)

Ultra-light axion (mass ~10-22 eV) with de Broglie wavelength on galaxy scales. Quantum pressure prevents small structure from forming.

SIDM

Self-Interacting Dark Matter — The Social Particle

Analogy: Most dark matter theories say particles pass through each other like ghosts. SIDM says they actually bump into each other (like billiard balls), which smooths out the centers of clumps from pointy peaks to flat-topped hills.
  • Mass function: same as CDM (same number of clumps!)
  • Density: isothermal core + NFW envelope (cored, not cuspy)
  • Cross-section: 0.1–100 cm2/g (interaction strength)
  • Core formation: Kaplinghat et al. (2016)
  • Impact signature: same rate as CDM, but slightly softer perturbation

Same subhalo abundance as CDM but different internal structure (cored vs. cuspy). Because the perturbation signal is nearly identical at Gaia precision, we merge CDM+SIDM into one classification class.

Model Discrimination Power

Projected (illustrative) discrimination power between models, based on expected Bayes factors. Higher = more distinguishable. These values are design projections, not measured results. CDM+SIDM vs. suppressed models is the strongest expected signal. WDM vs. FDM discrimination is expected to be difficult because they produce similar suppression patterns. CDM and SIDM are merged into one class because the impulse approximation produces nearly identical stream perturbations.

Target Stellar Streams

Our seven cosmic rulers: We've selected seven stellar streams observed by the Gaia space telescope. Each is a long, thin trail of stars — the remains of a star cluster that was torn apart by the Milky Way's gravity over billions of years. Together, they cover different lengths, distances, and ages, giving us complementary views of the dark matter substructure around our galaxy.

Stream Properties

Blue bars show the angular length of each stream in degrees (how far across the sky it stretches). Orange bars show distance from Earth in kiloparsecs (1 kpc = 3,260 light-years). Longer streams interact with more dark matter, making them more sensitive detectors. Closer streams have more precisely measured star motions.

GD-1

The workhorse. The longest and best-studied Milky Way stream (~60 degrees, spanning a quarter of the sky). Multiple confirmed gaps and a "spur" feature (a clump of stars kicked sideways). Provides our strongest sensitivity to low-mass subhalos. At only ~12 kpc away, Gaia measures its stars with excellent precision.

Pal 5

The classic. Tidal tails of the Palomar 5 globular cluster. At 11 Gyr old, it's had the most time to accumulate perturbations. Shorter (~20 deg) but with prominent density variations that have already been used to constrain dark matter (Bonaca et al. 2019). Its old age compensates for its shorter length.

Orphan-Chenab

The giant. The widest stream in our sample (~120 degrees). Its enormous length gives it the highest geometric cross-section for subhalo encounters. Farther away (~20 kpc), so proper motion measurements are noisier, but the sheer length provides statistical power that no other stream can match.

ATLAS

The thin needle. A short (~15 deg) but extremely thin stream discovered in the ATLAS survey. Its very low velocity dispersion (stars moving almost identically) makes it exquisitely sensitive to perturbations — even a small subhalo creates a detectable gap. Think of it as a very sensitive instrument with a small field of view.

Jhelum

The double. A stream with two parallel components (a "bifurcation"). This complex morphology may itself be evidence of a subhalo interaction — a dark matter clump could have split the stream in two. At ~13 kpc, Gaia measures it well. The double structure provides unique constraints on impact geometry.

Fjorm

The clean one. A recently discovered thin stream at intermediate distance (~15 kpc). Its clean kinematics (stars moving very uniformly) make gap identification straightforward. Fewer confounding factors from the Milky Way's baryonic structures (spiral arms, giant molecular clouds).

Sylgr

The compact. The shortest stream in our sample (~12 deg) with fewer member stars. While this limits its statistical power individually, its very cold kinematics amplify sensitivity to small perturbations. Every stream adds information to the hierarchical analysis, even short ones.
